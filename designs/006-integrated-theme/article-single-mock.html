Hellman v. Craven and the Fight for Private-School Special-Education Access

Sep 8, 202612 min readParent Data Force14 sources
──────────── ✦ ────────────

At the intersection of special-education law, religious liberty, and the constitutional rules that divide federal and state power sits a case that may seem narrow but carries consequences far beyond the two families who brought it. Hellman v. Craven, docket number 26-97, now pending before the Supreme Court of the United States, asks a question that cuts to the heart of what it means to choose private schooling in the twenty-first century: whether a state may make a family’s right to educate its child in a private institution — including a religiously affiliated institution — contingent on giving up access to a benefit the state otherwise makes available.

The question is framed through a legal doctrine called the “unconstitutional conditions doctrine,” and it reaches back to a century-old decision, Pierce v. Society of Sisters, 268 U.S. 510 (1925), that established the right of parents to direct the education of their children and to enroll them in private schools. The Massachusetts regulation at issue does not forbid private schooling; indeed, it acknowledges that private-school students retain a right to certain publicly funded special-education services. But it insists, according to the lawsuit, that those services be delivered only in a public-school building or at a “neutral” location — never inside the private school the parents chose. For two Massachusetts families whose children with disabilities attend Jewish day schools, that restriction is not a technicality. It is, in their telling, a penalty placed on a constitutional right.

The case arrived at the Supreme Court through the certiorari process and, as of the research window of August 31–September 8, 2026, had been distributed for the Court’s confidential conference scheduled for September 28, 2026 — a strong signal that the Justices are considering granting review. The docket carries a full set of amicus briefs from advocacy groups, state governments, and individual scholars, a pattern that suggests the Court sees the matter as carrying broad significance. The outcome will determine not only whether these families can have their children taught on the grounds of a private school, but how far a state can go in using its funding levers to shape the choices parents make about where their children are educated.

This article explains the case, the regulation, the legal doctrines underpinning it, the procedural history and docket timeline, the amicus landscape, and the practical and federalism consequences of a grant or a denial. It draws on the docket timeline published by SCOTUSblog and the factual framing reported by K-12 Dive, and it relies on well-established general knowledge about Supreme Court mechanics and the relevant constitutional law.

For readers less familiar with the field, a few foundational concepts are worth defining up front. “Certiorari” is a Latin term meaning “to be informed” or “to be made certain”; a petition for a writ of certiorari is a formal request that a higher court review a lower court’s decision. “Cert” is simply shorthand for that petition. “Amicus curiae,” often shortened to “amicus,” means “friend of the court,” and an amicus brief is a filing by a non-party who has a strong interest in the outcome and wishes to influence the Court’s reasoning. “FAPE,” the “freely available appropriate public education,” is the central guarantee of the IDEA. These terms recur throughout the article; this introduction is meant to make the legal analysis legible regardless of the reader’s background.

The Families and the Underlying Dispute

According to reporting by Kara Arundel for K-12 Dive, published August 28, 2026 and updated August 31, 2026, the lawsuit was brought by two Massachusetts families whose children with disabilities attend Jewish day schools. The families sought publicly funded special-education services for their children. Massachusetts law, consistent with the federal Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), makes such services available to students who attend private schools — but the state draws a line about where those services may be delivered.

The families were not asking to enroll their children in public schools. They had chosen Jewish day schools, and they wanted their children to receive the publicly funded special-education support at those schools. Under the Massachusetts policy as described in the lawsuit, the answer was no: the services could be provided only at a public school or at a “neutral” location, not inside the private school.

This framing matters because it separates the issue from a simpler dispute about whether private-school students get special-education services at all. They do. Federal law requires “child find” obligations and a “individualized” set of services for children with disabilities who attend private schools, through what is called the “individual services” or “thirty percent” funding mechanism under IDEA. The dispute here is narrower and more pointed: the location of service delivery. And the families argue that the location restriction is not a neutral administrative choice but a condition that forces them to choose between their constitutional right to send their child to a private — in this case, Jewish — day school and their child’s right to the publicly funded services the state has elected to provide.

The emphasis on Jewish day schools is not incidental. It places the case squarely in the lineage of cases involving religious schooling and state interaction, a lineage that includes Pierce v. Society of Sisters itself (a Catholic school case) and the more recent Carson v. Makin and Spencer v. Doolittle decisions dealing with public funding of religious education. The families’ claim is that the state may not use the delivery of special-education services as a lever to pressure families out of religious private schooling.

Why the Location Rule Matters to These Parents

For a child with a disability, the setting in which services are delivered is often the difference between meaningful access and nominal access. Related services under IDEA — speech therapy, occupational therapy, counseling, and the like — are typically provided by specialized personnel. If a state routes those services to a public school building several miles away, the practical burden on a family can be substantial: additional transportation, scheduling conflicts, missed instructional time, and the erosion of the specialized, low-stress environment that a particular private school may have been chosen to provide.

The families’ constitutional argument is that Massachusetts has created a situation in which the benefit — publicly funded special-education services — is conditioned on abandoning the very reason they chose their private school. If the services are available only off-campus, then the private school, including its religious character and its disability-supportive environment, becomes functionally unavailable to the child. In the families’ framing, that is the essence of an unconstitutional condition: the state may not offer a benefit that effectively coerces the surrender of a constitutional right.

The Massachusetts Regulation at Issue

The regulation at issue, as framed in the petition and reporting, permits private-school students with disabilities to access publicly funded special-education services — but only at a public school or at a “neutral” location. It does not permit those services to be delivered on the grounds of the private school the parents selected.
